The problem

An XRP holder cannot use a Flare dApp.

Not "finds it hard". Cannot — without first becoming a different kind of user. Flare's Smart Accounts already let an XRPL payment drive contract calls. What was missing is everything between a dApp's intent and a payment a wallet will sign.

A second wallet

An EVM wallet, installed and funded, before anything can be signed.

A gas token

FLR acquired from somewhere, held only to pay for the privilege of transacting.

A bridge

Assets moved across, with the custody and timing risk that implies.

A new signature model

EVM transactions to read and approve, in a format XRPL users have never seen.

Each is small. Together they are a wall between the largest retail holder base in crypto and every application Flare has. The user already has a wallet. It is simply the wrong one.

The solution

Put the intent in 42 bytes.

An XRPL payment can carry a memo, and a memo is small. A real operation — targets, calldata, values, nonce, gas — is not. So the ledger carries only a commitment, and the operation travels off-ledger to an executor.

What actually runs

1024 bytes

What the ledger records

42 bytes

The ledger carries one byte in 24.

0xFE1 byteOpcode — committed execute
walletId1 byteWhich personal account acts
feeUBA8 bytesWhat the executor is paid
userOpHash32 byteskeccak256 — the commitment
total42 bytesWhatever the operation does

The commitment is what makes this safe rather than merely compact. An executor that submits anything other than the exact committed bytes produces a different hash, and the contract rejects it. The executor is paid to relay, never trusted to choose.

How it works

One payment, six steps, no further input.

The user signs once and stops participating. Everything after is machinery — and the executor is a separate actor that has to notice the payment on its own.

1

A dApp describes what it wants

Ordinary EVM calls — here approve then deposit. It never learns XRPL is involved.

2

The SDK assembles and commits

Calls become a PackedUserOperation; its hash goes into a 42-byte memo.

3

The user signs one payment

In Xaman it looks like any payment — an amount and a destination. Nothing unfamiliar.

4

FAssets mints and dispatches

Direct minting turns the payment into FXRP and forwards the instruction.

5

The Data Connector proves it

An XRPPayment attestation finalises in a voting round; a Merkle proof is fetched.

6

The operation executes

executeUserOp checks the hash and runs the batch — from an account that did not exist when the payment was signed.

Step 5 is load-bearing. Nobody has to trust the relay. Flare verifies the XRPL payment through its own oracle, and the commitment binds the relay to the approved operation.

What was built

An SDK, a relay, and the contracts to prove it.

Flare supplied the primitives. ONESIG is the packaging that makes them reachable from a dApp — and filling the gaps found along the way.

Built new

  • SDK — encoders for all seven Smart Accounts memo opcodes, byte layouts transcribed from MemoInstructions.sol.
  • Executor — the FDC attestation pipeline as a resumable state machine, so a crash mid-round recovers instead of losing the job.
  • Contracts — InstructionRegistry, plus Counter, MockFXRP and MockVault as demo targets.
  • Demo surface — Xaman sign-in, live pipeline, three explorer links that fill only once earned.

Integrated, not written

  • Flare Smart Accounts — the 0xFE instruction, MasterAccountController, PersonalAccount.executeUserOp.
  • FAssets v1.3 — direct minting, so one payment both mints FXRP and dispatches the memo.
  • Flare Data Connector — the XRPPayment attestation type.
  • Contract Registry — every address resolved at runtime, never copied from docs.

Five of the seven memo opcodes are absent from the developer documentation. Every encoder asserts the exact length the contract's require() enforces — so a malformed memo fails locally, instead of after a payment is signed and a nonce burned.
